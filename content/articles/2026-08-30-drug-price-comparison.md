The original story reported the manufacturer's list price increase — 8%, the third such increase
in two years — and left it there, implying patients will pay 8% more at the pharmacy counter. For
most insured patients, that's not how drug pricing works, and the piece didn't explain why.

List price is what a manufacturer nominally charges before rebates negotiated by insurers and
pharmacy benefit managers, which for many high-list-price drugs run 40–60% off the sticker number.
For the large majority of insured patients on this drug, co-pay is set by their plan's formulary
tier, not the list price directly, and CMS data shows the average net price for this drug class has
been roughly flat over the same two-year period the list price climbed.

The people this does hit hard are the uninsured and those in high-deductible plans before they meet
their deductible, where list price is closer to what's actually charged. That's a real and
important story. It's a different story from "the price patients pay is rising," which is what the
original headline implied.
