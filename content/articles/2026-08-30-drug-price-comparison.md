The original story reported the manufacturer's list price increase — 8%, the third such increase
in two years — and left it there, implying patients will pay 8% more at the pharmacy counter. For
most insured patients, that's not how drug pricing works, and the piece didn't explain why,
because it never mentioned the distinction between list price and net price at all.

## Two prices, not one

Every branded prescription drug effectively has two prices. The list price (also called the
wholesale acquisition cost) is the sticker number the manufacturer nominally charges. The net
price is what actually changes hands after rebates negotiated by insurers and pharmacy benefit
managers (PBMs) — and for many high-list-price drugs, those rebates run 40–60% off the sticker
number. A manufacturer can raise the list price and simultaneously increase rebates enough that
the net price barely moves, which is closer to what has happened here.

For the large majority of insured patients on this drug, co-pay is set by the health plan's
formulary tier, not by the list price directly — a patient on a $35 tier-2 co-pay pays $35 whether
the list price is $4,200 or $4,536. CMS's own tracking shows the average net price for this drug
class has been roughly flat over the same two-year period the list price climbed 8% a third time.

## Who this framing serves, and who it misleads

List-price increases are the number manufacturers report to the SEC and the number that shows up
in press releases, so they're the easiest number for a reporter to get quickly — but they're also
the number that most overstates what's happening to what patients actually pay. A few figures that
would have made the original piece more accurate:

- List price increase reported: 8% (accurate, as far as it goes)
- Estimated average net price change for insured patients on standard formularies: approximately
  flat, per CMS data
- Share of prescriptions for this drug filled by patients on commercial insurance: roughly 70%
- Share filled by patients who are uninsured or in a high-deductible plan before meeting their
  deductible: roughly 15%, the group for whom list price is closest to what's actually charged

## Where the real story is

The people this genuinely does hit hard are the uninsured and those in high-deductible health
plans before they meet their deductible, where list price is much closer to what's actually
charged at the counter — an 8% increase on a $4,200 drug is a real and painful jump for that group.
Patients using manufacturer coupon programs can also see effects if the coupon caps don't scale
with list price. That's a real, specific, and important story about a real gap in how U.S. drug
pricing protects (or fails to protect) different groups of patients differently.

It's a different story from "the price patients pay is rising," which is what the original
headline implied for a much broader group than it actually applies to. The distinction isn't a
technicality — it's the difference between a story about a pricing system that mostly absorbs list
price increases for insured patients, and a story about a system that doesn't. Only one of those
is what the data shows for most people affected.
