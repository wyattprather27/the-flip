The original article opened with two recent, high-profile incidents in the same neighborhood over
one weekend and described the city as gripped by a "crime wave." The incidents were real and
serious. The trend line the phrase implies is not, and the piece never checked.

## What "wave" actually implies, and what the data shows

"Wave" is a trend word — it claims things are getting worse, and getting worse in a sustained way.
The city police department's own year-to-date data, which the original piece did not cite, shows
violent crime down 12% compared to the same period last year. That's not a one-quarter blip: it's
the third consecutive year of decline, following a spike in 2022–2023 that the city is still
recovering from in the public narrative even as the numbers have moved on.

Breaking the 12% figure down by category:

- Homicides: down 18% year over year, continuing a decline since the 2023 peak
- Robbery: down 9%
- Aggravated assault: down 11%
- Property crime (not part of the "violent crime" figure, but often conflated with it in
  coverage): down 6%

Every major category moved the same direction. That matters, because it rules out the possibility
that the aggregate number is being flattered by one category improving while others worsen.

## Why a bad weekend doesn't reverse a three-year trend

Two incidents in one weekend can happen — and did happen — during a multi-year decline. Monthly
and even weekly crime counts are naturally noisy; a city with a few hundred violent incidents a
year will have some weekends with zero and some with several, purely from statistical variance, even
with no change in the underlying rate. Treating a single weekend as a trend reversal requires
ignoring how count data works. Criminologists typically recommend looking at rolling 12-month
windows for exactly this reason — it's long enough to smooth out random clustering but short
enough to catch a genuine turn if one is happening.

By that standard, there is no turn happening. The 12-month rolling violent crime rate has declined
in 34 of the last 36 months tracked by the city's CompStat dashboard.

## What actually deserves scrutiny here

This isn't a claim that crime is a non-issue, that the two incidents don't deserve coverage, or
that residents' fear is invalid — fear and statistics are different things, and a decline in the
citywide rate doesn't erase a bad night in a specific neighborhood. A story about *why* certain
neighborhoods still see clusters of violent incidents even as the citywide rate falls would be a
genuinely useful piece of journalism — it might point to under-policing, under-investment, or a
handful of repeat offenders driving a disproportionate share of incidents in one area. That's a
sharper, more specific story than "crime wave," and it's the one the original piece had the
material to write but didn't.
